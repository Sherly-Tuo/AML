Research question

Can ML-based dynamic pricing in peer-to-peer renewable energy markets systematically improve household seller revenue compared to a fixed grid feed-in tariff (FiT)?

Using Victoria (VIC1) electricity market data and Melbourne solar generation records, we train an OLS baseline and a Random Forest demand model, then run a grid search over 48 candidate prices to find the listing price that maximises expected profit relative to the FiT opportunity cost. Our results show that ML-optimised prices outperform the FiT baseline in expected net revenue across all tested hours, with the largest gains during high-demand, low-supply periods — suggesting that algorithmic pricing can meaningfully improve P2P market participation incentives for rooftop solar households.

Policy relevance: If P2P pricing incentives are large enough, households are more likely to invest in rooftop solar and actively share energy with neighbours — accelerating community-level clean energy adoption without central grid subsidy.

Part 0

Introduction

Solar energy and decentralised clean-energy trading are becoming increasingly important as power systems shift away from centralised fossil-fuel dependence. Yet traditional electricity allocation remains inflexible: small-scale households can produce excess solar generation, but have limited ability to price, trade, or reallocate that energy efficiently within local communities.

VoltShare addresses this gap by asking how applied machine learning can support smarter local energy exchange. It uses historical demand-side and supply-side data, hourly weather context, and predictive pricing logic to recommend a listing price for surplus renewable energy. The AML component combines an interpretable OLS baseline with a Random Forest comparison model, then evaluates candidate prices through an expected-revenue optimisation step.

In parallel, we built a full-stack web application to simulate how these model outputs could function inside a real user-facing marketplace. The result is both an academic AML exercise and a product prototype at the intersection of climate technology, data science, and digital market design.

Core purpose: show how applied ML, predictive pricing, and a user-facing web application can be combined to address a real-world clean-energy allocation problem.

Part 1

Data pipeline

VoltShare does not read raw CSVs directly. Instead, it generates structured hourly databases, then joins them by timestamp before modelling and recommendation.

Demand database

VIC1 price-demand file → hourly normalised demand + buyer-side willingness-to-pay proxy. Used to train OLS and Random Forest.

Source: AEMO price_and_demand_vic1.csv

Supply database

Quarter-hour solar generation → hourly household-scale surplus estimates + supply-side reference prices.

Source: APVI Solar_Energy_Generation.csv

Weather context

Historical hourly weather (temperature, cloud cover, radiation, precipitation) aligned to Melbourne. Affects both demand and supply adjustments.

Source: Open-Meteo archive API

Pipeline: VIC1 rows → hourly demand bids → feature engineering → OLS + RF training → grid-search pricing → recommended listing price. The optimisation context joins time features, lag features, supply context, and weather context at the same hourly timestamp.

Supply price vs demand bid — by hour of day

The gap between these two lines is the pricing margin the algorithm navigates. When demand bids exceed supply price, there is room to raise the listing price.

Part 2

Demand side

The demand side estimates buyer willingness to pay by transforming regional VIC1 data into hourly proxy bids. VIC1 is the Victorian regional electricity market identifier in the NEM.

Demand quantity formula

Dₜ = mean(TOTALDEMANDₜ) / 1000

Average VIC1 demand within each hour, rescaled to household-level quantity.

Demand-side price proxy

Pₜ = clamp( Q₀.₉₀(RRPₜ⁺) / 1000 , 0.02 , 0.40 )

Upper-quantile positive RRP used as willingness-to-pay proxy. Unit converted from $/MWh to $/kWh.

OLS baseline (interpretable model)

log(Ďₜ) = β₀ + β₁pₜ + β₂hourSinₜ + β₃hourCosₜ + β₄monthSinₜ + β₅monthCosₜ + β₆lagDemand1ₜ + β₇lagDemand24ₜ + β₈lagPrice1ₜ + ...

OLS predicts log(demand) using price, cyclical time encodings, and lag features. Interpretable baseline for the coursework.

Random Forest (nonlinear extension)

Ďₜ = f( pₜ, hourₜ, monthₜ, dayOfWeekₜ, lagDemand1ₜ, lagDemand24ₜ, lagPrice1ₜ, rollingDemand24ₜ, logPrice_x_hourSinₜ, weatherₜ, ... )

Random Forest captures nonlinear interactions (e.g. price × time-of-day) that OLS cannot express linearly.

Average demand by hour of day

Shows when buyer-side pressure peaks — the model learns this intraday pattern from lag and rolling features.

Feature engineering

Time features: sin/cos encodings of hour (24h cycle) and month (12-month cycle), plus weekend indicator.
Lag features: lagDemand1, lagDemand24, lagPrice1, lagPrice24 (1-hour and 1-day lags).
Rolling features: 24-hour rolling mean of demand and price.
Interaction features (RF only): logPrice × hourSin, logPrice × hourCos, lagDemandDelta, lagPriceDelta.

Part 3

Supply side

The supply side approximates what a typical household could list by aggregating multi-site solar generation into hourly surplus reference quotes.

Site-hour generation

G₍s,t₎ = Σⱼ max( SolarGeneration₍s,t,j₎ , 0 )

Sum of positive quarter-hour solar generation per site per hour. Only positive generation retained.

Household surplus proxy

Sₜ = medianₛ( G₍s,t₎ ) / 4

Hourly median across active sites, divided by 4 to scale from fleet-level to typical household surplus.

Supply-side price reference

Priceₜ = 0.19 − normalizedAbundanceₜ × 0.07 clamped to [0.11, 0.19]

Inversely mapped from hourly abundance: higher surplus → lower reference price; tighter supply → higher price.

Weather-aware supply adjustment

ΔSₜ = clamp( (radiationₜ − 240)·0.0026 + (DNIₜ − 300)·0.0014 − cloudₜ·0.018 − rainₜ·0.55 + nightPenalty , −0.9Sₜ , 0.9Sₜ )

Radiation lifts available supply; cloud, rain, and night-time conditions reduce it.

Model evaluation

Both models use forward time-split (train/validation/test at 70%/80%/100%). No random shuffling — order preserved.

Weather ablation finding: Adding Melbourne historical weather did not significantly improve test-set accuracy (RF with weather: R²=0.9251 vs without: R²=0.9272). This suggests the current demand signal is already well-explained by temporal and lag features. A single-location weather proxy may be too coarse for a regional (VIC1) demand series — a direction for future work.

Part 5

Grid-search price optimisation

Rather than outputting a single market price, VoltShare evaluates 48 candidate prices and selects the one with the highest expected net profit.

FiT opportunity cost proxy

FiTₜ = clamp( Q₀.₁₅(supply pricesₜ) , 0.02 , 0.30 )

The 15th percentile of nearby supply prices acts as the floor: the cost of using P2P rather than grid feed-in tariff.

Retail tariff proxy (shortfall penalty)

Retailₜ = clamp( max(Q₀.₈₅(demand bidsₜ), FiTₜ + 0.03) , FiTₜ + 0.03 , 0.45 )

Buyer's outside option: if P2P price exceeds this, they buy from the grid instead.

Market share (logistic response)

shareₜ(p) = 1 / (1 + exp( k · (p − midpoint) )) where midpoint = (FiT + Retail) / 2 k = 8 / (Retail − FiT)

Low price → high buyer share; price ≥ retail tariff → 0% share. Reflects price-sensitive buyer behaviour.

Expected profit per scenario

profit = ownSupplyUsed · (p − FiT) − shortfall · max(Retail − p, 0) p* = argmaxₚ E[profit]

Averaged over demand and supply residual samples. The price maximising expected profit is recommended.

Grid search — expected profit across candidate prices (illustrative)

The algorithm evaluates each candidate price and selects the peak. The orange line marks FiT (floor); the red dashed line marks the retail tariff (ceiling).

Part 6

Key findings & limitations

Key findings

  • Random Forest outperforms OLS across all metrics (R² 0.927 vs 0.899)
  • ML-optimised prices consistently exceed FiT baseline in expected net revenue
  • Price gains are largest during high-demand, low-supply hours (evening peaks)
  • Temporal and lag features dominate demand prediction; weather adds little
  • Grid-search pricing provides a principled, interpretable optimisation step

Limitations

  • VIC1 is regional market data, not real household P2P bids
  • Weather proxy is a single Melbourne location — too coarse for regional series
  • No postcode/meter-level validation of trading eligibility
  • Supply proxy from fleet solar, not individual rooftop meters
  • All prices and profits are simulated, not real transactions

Policy implication: If dynamic pricing via ML increases expected seller revenue by even a modest margin, it lowers the break-even threshold for rooftop solar investment — creating a market-based incentive for households to participate in local clean energy sharing without requiring new grid infrastructure or government subsidy.
